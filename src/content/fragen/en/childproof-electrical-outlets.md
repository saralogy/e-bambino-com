Toddlers most often get shocked when they bite an electrical cord or poke a metal object, such as a fork or a knife, into an unprotected outlet or appliance. Outlet accidents are typical from about 7 months to 4 years, so the best time to childproof is before your baby starts to crawl.

## When should I start covering outlets?

Before your baby is on the move. Outlet accidents become typical from about 7 months, as babies start to explore on their own, and stay a risk until about age 4. If you are setting up a [nursery in a small apartment](/nursery/nursery-in-a-small-apartment/), plan furniture around the outlets at the same time.

## What makes a good outlet cover?

A good cover is hard for a child to remove and large enough that a child cannot choke on it. Test each one by trying to pull it out yourself, and replace any that come loose easily. A small cap that a toddler can pry out becomes a choking hazard of its own.

## Are tamper-resistant outlets better than covers?

They are the more permanent fix, and CPSC recommends tamper-resistant receptacles whenever outlets are replaced. They open only when a plug is inserted, so there is no loose part to remove, lose or forget to put back.

## Which outlets and cords are easy to miss?

Power strips and outdoor outlets need protection too, not just the wall outlets in the nursery. Keep electrical cords out of your child's reach and sight, since biting a cord is a common cause of shocks. If your child keeps going back to one outlet, move a heavy piece of furniture in front of it.

## What is a GFCI, and do I need to test it?

A GFCI (ground fault circuit interrupter) is a safety outlet or breaker that cuts power when current leaks, and CPSC notes it prevents many electrocutions. Test each GFCI monthly with its test button, following the manufacturer's instructions.

## What should I do if my child gets an electric shock?

If your child is still in contact with the current, cut the power first, by unplugging the device or switching off the main, and never touch a live wire with bare hands; if you must move a wire away from your child, use something dry and nonmetallic. Once the power is off, check breathing and pulse, and if your child is not breathing or has no heartbeat, start CPR right away while someone calls 911. Move your child as little as possible. Even if your child seems fine, have them seen by a pediatrician, because a shock can cause internal damage, and burns in the mouth from biting a cord are often deeper than they look.

| Spot | What to do |
|---|---|
| Unused wall outlets | Cover them with covers a child cannot easily remove and cannot choke on |
| Outlets being replaced | Install tamper-resistant receptacles |
| Power strips | Fit child locks or keep them out of reach |
| Outdoor outlets | Secure them like indoor outlets |
| Cords | Keep out of reach and sight, especially where a child could chew them |
| An outlet your child keeps returning to | Block it with heavy furniture |
| GFCI outlets | Press the test button once a month |
